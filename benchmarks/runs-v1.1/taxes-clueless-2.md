# Freelance designer (US, year one) — bookkeeping & tax setup

## Do this

**Step 1 — Separate your money today (30 min, free).**
Open a second checking account at whatever bank you already use, label it "business." Every payment from a client lands there first, nothing else does. Once a week, move your "pay" out to personal checking. This one habit is 80% of bookkeeping — it means your books are just "what's in this account," not a forensic reconstruction of a mixed account in April.

**Step 2 — Get a bookkeeping tool that talks to that account (15 min).**
Sign up for **Wave** (free, wave.apps.intuit.com) — not QuickBooks. Connect the business checking account (read-only bank feed). Wave will pull in every transaction automatically; you just tag each one "income" or an expense category (software, home office, travel, etc.) once a week. 10 minutes, Sunday night, recurring calendar event — set that now.

*Decided for you further down explains why Wave over QuickBooks/spreadsheet.*

**Step 3 — Set aside tax money the moment you get paid, automatically.**
As a freelancer, no one withholds tax for you — the IRS expects you to pay it yourself, four times a year, and if you don't, you owe a penalty even if you pay in full by April. The safe rule with no math required: **the instant a client payment clears, move 30% (estimate — see Careful below) of it into a separate savings account called "taxes." Never touch that account for anything else.** Set up an automatic bank rule or just do it by hand within 24 hours of each payment — put a recurring reminder in your phone triggered by "got paid."

**Step 4 — Pay quarterly estimated taxes, on autopilot dates.**
Every quarter, log into **IRS Direct Pay** (irs.gov/payments/direct-pay, free, no account needed, pay straight from your bank) and pay from that "taxes" account. 2026 due dates for 2026 income: **Apr 15, Jun 15, Sep 15, 2026, and Jan 15, 2027.** Put all four in your calendar right now, titled "estimated taxes — pay from tax savings account." Also check your state — most states with income tax (e.g., CA, NY) want their own quarterly estimate too; states with no income tax (TX, FL, WA, etc.) don't. Tell me your state if you want the exact state-specific link and rate.

**Step 5 — Get an EIN (free, 10 minutes, do it even though you don't strictly need one).**
Go to irs.gov, apply for a free EIN (Employer Identification Number) as a sole proprietor. This lets you give clients an EIN instead of your Social Security Number on the W-9 forms they'll ask you to fill out — putting your SSN in a client's file cabinet for years is the kind of thing that comes back to bite you in a breach. Costs nothing, takes 10 minutes, do it before your next client asks for a W-9.

**Step 6 — Every new client: send a W-9 before the first invoice, keep every invoice.**
When a client asks "can you fill out a W-9," that's normal — it's how they report what they paid you. Use your new EIN on it, not your SSN. Keep a copy of every invoice you send and every W-9 you send, in one folder (Google Drive folder named "2026 freelance," subfolders "invoices sent" and "W-9s sent"). In January 2027, clients who paid you $600+ will send you a **1099-NEC** — keep those in the same folder; you'll need them to check your own numbers match what they report to the IRS.

**Step 7 — Track deductible expenses as you go, not in April.**
In Wave, tag these as they happen (all are legitimately deductible for a freelance designer): software subscriptions (Adobe, Figma, fonts), a proportional share of your home internet if you work from home, a business-use percentage of a laptop/monitor/tablet, the home office deduction if you have a dedicated workspace, and mileage/travel to client meetings. Don't try to reconstruct this from memory later — tag it the week it happens.

## Careful

- **The 30% tax set-aside is an estimate, not a real number for you** — actual freelance tax is roughly 15.3% self-employment tax (Social Security + Medicare) plus your income tax bracket on top. For a first-year designer with modest income, effective rate often lands 20–30%, but it depends on your total income, state, and deductions. **Confirm your real number with a CPA or the IRS's Form 1040-ES worksheet before your first quarterly payment in Q1 2026** — if you're under-set-aside, you find out in April with a bill you can't pay; if you're over, you just get a refund, which is the safer side to err on.
- **Missing a quarterly payment date isn't "pay it later, no big deal" — it accrues a penalty from that date forward**, even if your final return is correct and paid in full. The calendar reminders in Step 4 are not optional flavor — they're the difference between a normal tax year and an unpleasant one.
- **Mixing business and personal money in one account is the #1 thing that turns an audit into a nightmare** — if you skip Step 1, you will spend hours next March trying to remember which of 200 transactions were business, and the IRS doesn't take your word for it. Do Step 1 before you take your next payment, not after.
- **1099-NECs you receive are informational — the IRS also gets a copy.** If your own income tracking doesn't add up to what your 1099s say, that's exactly the kind of mismatch that triggers an automated notice. This is why Step 6's folder matters.

## Check it worked

At the end of your first full week: open Wave, look at the business checking account feed — every transaction should be tagged (income or an expense category), nothing sitting "uncategorized." If something's uncategorized, your weekly 10-minute habit isn't happening yet — fix that before it piles up.

## Decided for you

- **Chose Wave over QuickBooks** because it's free for your transaction volume (QuickBooks starts charging immediately and is overkill for one person with no employees) and over a spreadsheet because a spreadsheet requires you to remember to enter things — Wave's bank feed means you can't forget a transaction, you can only forget to tag it.
- **Chose a separate savings account over a tax software's "safe harbor" percentage calculator** as the default mechanism, because a simple physical separation of money ("that account is not mine to spend") works even on days you don't look at any software, and software calculators are easy to ignore.
- **Chose sole proprietor + EIN over forming an LLC or S-corp** for year one, because entity formation costs money and adds filing complexity with no tax benefit at typical freelance-designer income levels in year one — this is worth revisiting with a CPA once you're consistently clearing $60k+ (estimate, confirm with a CPA) a year, where an S-corp election can reduce self-employment tax.
- **Chose IRS Direct Pay over mailing checks or using a payment app** because it's free, instant, and gives you a confirmation number as proof of payment — mailed checks can get lost with no easy proof you paid.
