# Do this

**Step 1 — Open a separate business checking account, this week.**
Any free business checking account works (Novo, Found, or your existing bank's free business tier). Move every client payment into this account and pay every business expense from it. Never mix personal and business money in one account — this single habit is what makes everything below possible.

**Step 2 — Set aside 30% of every payment the moment it lands.**
Open a separate high-yield savings account labeled "taxes — do not touch." Every time a client pays you, immediately transfer 30% (estimate, confirm with your CPA — see below) of that payment into it. This money is not yours; you are holding it for the IRS and your state. 30% is a standard freelancer rule-of-thumb blending self-employment tax (~15.3%) plus federal and state income tax at typical first-year income levels.

**Step 3 — Track income and expenses with one free tool: a spreadsheet, not an app.**
Make a copy of a simple income/expense tracker (Google Sheets, one tab "Income" with columns Date / Client / Amount / Invoice#, one tab "Expenses" with columns Date / Vendor / Amount / Category / Business purpose). Log every payment and every business purchase (software, a portion of your laptop, a portion of home internet, client-related travel) as it happens — five minutes, weekly, not a year-end scramble.

**Step 4 — Save every receipt digitally.**
Photograph or forward every business-expense receipt to a dedicated folder (a Google Drive folder called "2026 Tax Receipts" is enough). The IRS can audit up to 3 years back (6 if they think you underreported by a lot). Keep records that long, not just until filing.

**Step 5 — Pay quarterly estimated taxes.**
Because no employer withholds tax for you, the IRS wants you to pay as you earn, four times a year. 2026 payment due dates (estimate, confirm at irs.gov/payments — these shift slightly year to year):
- Q1 (Jan–Mar income): due April 15, 2026
- Q2 (Apr–May income): due June 15, 2026
- Q3 (Jun–Aug income): due September 15, 2026
- Q4 (Sep–Dec income): due January 15, 2027

Pay directly at IRS.gov/payments (Direct Pay, free, no account needed) using the money sitting in your "taxes" savings account. Do the same for your state if your state has income tax (most do — check your state's department of revenue site; a few states like Texas and Florida have none).

**Step 6 — File Form 1040 + Schedule C + Schedule SE next spring.**
Schedule C reports your freelance income and expenses; Schedule SE calculates your self-employment tax. Use tax software that handles self-employment (FreeTaxUSA is free for federal, cheap for state, and handles Schedule C fine for a first-year freelancer with straightforward income — no need to pay for TurboTax's expensive tier unless your situation is unusually complex).

**Step 7 — Get an accountant on your calendar before January, not after.**
Even at "full clueless" mode, one paid hour with a CPA who handles freelancers (expect $150–300/hr, estimate, confirm locally) before your first tax season is worth more than any app. Ask them specifically: "Am I setting aside enough? Should I be an LLC or S-corp yet? Am I missing deductions?" A first-year freelancer almost never needs an LLC or S-corp yet — that's a later-stage optimization, not a now problem.

# Careful

- **Underpaying quarterly taxes triggers a penalty, even if you pay in full by April.** The IRS charges an underpayment penalty (roughly 8% annualized, estimate, confirm at irs.gov) if you don't pay enough each quarter. This is why Step 2 and Step 5 exist — don't skip the quarterly payments thinking "I'll just pay it all in April."
- **Commingling personal and business money is the single biggest thing that gets clueless freelancers in trouble.** If the IRS or a client dispute ever looks at your finances, one mixed account makes it impossible to prove what's a business expense. This is why Step 1 is first and non-negotiable.
- **Missing quarterly deadlines compounds.** Each missed quarter adds its own penalty clock. If you miss one, pay it as soon as you realize — don't wait for the next deadline to "catch up," that just adds more penalty.
- **Deleting or losing receipts is often irreversible** (a shut-down app, a dead phone). Step 4's rule — forward to cloud storage, don't leave receipts stranded in one app or one phone — is there for exactly this.

# Check it worked

At the end of this month, open your "taxes" savings account and confirm the balance equals roughly 30% of everything you were paid this month. If it's lower, you spent your tax money without meaning to — top it back up immediately, before you spend more.

# Decided for you

- **Separate business bank account over one shared account** — because proving business vs. personal expenses is the #1 way freelancers accidentally cost themselves deductions or invite scrutiny.
- **30% set-aside over a lower number** — because self-employment tax (15.3%) alone eats a big chunk before income tax is even added; 30% is a safe overestimate you can revise down with a CPA once you know your real bracket.
- **FreeTaxUSA over TurboTax** — because Schedule C for a straightforward freelance designer doesn't need a $100+ software tier; free federal filing plus ~$15 state covers it.
- **Sole proprietor (no LLC, no S-corp) for year one** — because forming an entity adds cost and complexity most first-year freelancers don't yet need; revisit this with a CPA once income is stable and meaningful (roughly $60k+/year is the usual point people start asking about S-corp election).
- **A plain spreadsheet over a paid bookkeeping app** — because at freelancer scale (a handful of clients, modest expenses) a spreadsheet costs nothing and does the job; upgrade to something like Wave or QuickBooks Self-Employed only if transaction volume gets unmanageable.

# Only you can decide

- **Which state you're in** — state income tax rules and deadlines vary (some states have none). Assumed: you're in a state with income tax and will check your state revenue department's site once, this week.
- **Whether you expect to owe more than ~$1,000 in tax this year** — if your freelance income this year is small (a few thousand dollars, side-gig scale), you may not owe quarterly estimated taxes at all. Assumed: your freelancing is meant to be a real income source, so the quarterly-payment system above applies.
- **Your rough annual income estimate** — this decides whether 30% is too much or too little to set aside. Assumed: modest first-year income (under ~$60k), where 30% is a safe, slightly conservative cushion.
