# Freelance designer tax/bookkeeping setup (US, year 1)

## Do this

**Step 0 (today, before you spend or deposit another client dollar): open a separate business checking account.** Any bank is fine — Novo, Found, Bluevine, or your regular bank's free business checking. This is the one step that must not wait. Every dollar you earn freelancing goes into this account first; every business expense comes out of it. Nothing else below works if income and personal spending are mixed in one account.

**Step 1: pick your bookkeeping tool — use a spreadsheet, not software, for year one.**
Make a copy of this structure (Google Sheets is fine, free, and good enough):

- **Tab "Income"**: columns = Date | Client | Description | Amount | Invoice # | Paid? (Y/N)
- **Tab "Expenses"**: columns = Date | Vendor | Description | Amount | Category (see list below) | Business % (see below)
- **Tab "Tax Savings"**: columns = Date | Income that week/month | Amount set aside (30%)

Categories to use for Expenses (these map to the tax form you'll file, Schedule C):
Software/subscriptions, Equipment (computer, monitor, tablet), Home office, Professional development (courses, books), Contract labor (if you pay anyone), Marketing/website, Bank/payment fees, Travel, Meals (client meetings only), Phone/internet (business %), Insurance.

**Step 2: every time you get paid, immediately move 30% (estimate — see Careful #1) of that payment into a separate high-yield savings account labeled "Taxes — do not touch."** This is not optional and not "later." Do it the same day the money lands. This one habit is what prevents the year-one disaster: owing $8,000 in April with $0 saved.

**Step 3: log every expense weekly**, 10 minutes, same day each week (put it on your calendar now). Photograph or forward every receipt to a dedicated email folder or a scanning app (even just a phone photos album called "Receipts 2026"). You don't need fancy software — you need the habit and the paper trail.

**Step 4: pay quarterly estimated taxes.** The IRS wants you to pay taxes as you earn, not once a year. Deadlines for 2026 tax year (pay via IRS Direct Pay, no account needed, free):
- Q1: April 15, 2026
- Q2: June 15, 2026
- Q3: September 15, 2026
- Q4: January 15, 2027

How much to pay each quarter: take your "Tax Savings" tab total since the last payment, and send roughly that amount. If you underpay by a lot you get hit with a penalty; if you overpay you get it back when you file. When in doubt, send more, not less.

**Step 5: get an invoice template with your info, client's info, amount, due date, and payment terms ("due within 15 days") locked in now** — use a free one from Wave or Google Docs. Every client gets one before you start work, not after.

**Step 6: file for an EIN (free, 5 minutes, irs.gov/ein) instead of giving clients your Social Security Number on W-9 forms.** Clients who pay you $600+/year will send you a Form W-9 to fill out and a Form 1099 at year-end. Put the EIN on the W-9, not your SSN — your SSN going to every client who hires you is an unnecessary leak.

**Step 7: at tax time (Jan–April 2027), hand your three spreadsheet tabs to a CPA or use TaxAct/FreeTaxUSA Self-Employed** ($30–70, estimate, confirm current price) instead of TurboTax's self-employed tier (pricier for the same form). You'll file Schedule C (business income/expenses) and Schedule SE (self-employment tax) alongside your regular Form 1040.

## Careful

- **The 30% isn't a real number for you specifically — it's a safe placeholder.** Your actual rate depends on total income, state, and filing status. If you clear more than ~$50k in 1099 income this year, your real rate could be higher; if this is a side income under ~$20k, it could be lower. Setting aside too much just sits in savings (recoverable); setting aside too little means an April bill you can't pay (not easily recoverable — the IRS charges penalties and interest on top). Default to 30% and adjust after you run one real projection (see "Only you can decide" #1).
- **Missing a quarterly payment isn't just "pay it next quarter."** The IRS charges an underpayment penalty calculated per quarter you missed, not just on the year-end total. If you miss Q1, pay what you owe as soon as you realize it — don't wait for Q2 to "catch up," that extends the penalty window.
- **Commingling funds (business and personal in one account) isn't just messy — if you ever form an LLC, it's the #1 reason courts pierce liability protection.** Keep the accounts separate from day one even before you decide on a business structure.
- **Losing receipts isn't fatal, but it is expensive.** Without documentation, the IRS can disallow a deduction in an audit even if the expense was real. The phone-photo habit in Step 3 is the fix; skipping it for "a few months to catch up later" is how people end up with a shoebox and a guess in April.

## Check it worked

At the end of your first full month of freelancing, your "Tax Savings" tab total should equal roughly 30% of everything in your "Income" tab for that month, actually sitting in the separate savings account — not just logged on paper. If the number in the sheet and the number in the bank don't match, the system has already broken and you've found it early, which is the whole point.

## Decided for you

- **Spreadsheet over QuickBooks/FreshBooks for year one** — because you said you don't want to learn a new system, and a $0 sheet with 3 tabs does everything a $30/month tool does until you have employees or inventory. Revisit once you're steady past ~$40k/year in revenue.
- **Separate savings account over "just leave it in checking"** — because money that's reachable gets spent; money in a separate account labeled "Taxes" almost never does.
- **EIN over SSN on client paperwork** — free, five minutes, one less place your SSN lives.
- **FreeTaxUSA/TaxAct over TurboTax** — same forms, same result, notably cheaper for self-employment filings (estimate, confirm current pricing before you file).
- **Cash accounting over accrual** — you count income when you're actually paid, not when you invoice. Simpler, and what almost every solo freelancer uses; the IRS default for a business your size.

## Only you can decide

1. **Your real tax-savings percentage.** I defaulted to 30% (estimate, confirm with your CPA). If you want a number tuned to you, run the free IRS Tax Withholding Estimator or TaxAct's quarterly calculator once you have ~2 months of real income data — assumed: you'll do this after month one and adjust the savings tab formula.
2. **Whether you need a business structure (LLC) at all yet.** Assumed: no, not yet — a sole proprietorship (which is what you are by default, no paperwork needed) is fine until your income is consistent and meaningful (people typically consider it past ~$40–60k/year in profit, estimate) or you want liability protection for client work. Revisit in 6–12 months, not now.
3. **Which state you're filing in and whether it has its own quarterly estimated tax requirement.** Assumed: you'll check your state's department of revenue site once, since about 40 states have income tax and most of those also want quarterly estimates separately from the federal ones above — this doc covers federal only.
