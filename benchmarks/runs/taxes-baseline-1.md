Totally doable — you don't need to become a bookkeeper, you just need a system that runs itself. Here's the simple setup:

## 1. Separate your money (do this first)
Open a **free business checking account** (or a dedicated personal checking account you only use for freelance work) at whatever bank you already use. All client payments go in, all business expenses come out of it. This one habit does 80% of the work of "bookkeeping" for you automatically, because your bank statement becomes your ledger.

## 2. Save for taxes as you go — the single most important habit
As a freelancer, no one withholds taxes for you, so you have to do it yourself or you'll owe a scary lump sum in April.

- **Every time you get paid, immediately move 25–30% of it into a separate high-yield savings account** labeled "Taxes — do not touch." Treat it as if it's not your money.
- Why 25–30%: you owe both income tax and self-employment tax (~15.3% for Social Security/Medicare) on your net profit. 25% is a safe default if you're a low-to-mid earner; bump to 30% if you expect to clear ~$60k+ profit this year.

## 3. Pay quarterly estimated taxes
The IRS expects payments 4x a year, not just once in April, if you expect to owe $1,000+.

- Due roughly: **April 15, June 15, September 15, January 15** (of the following year).
- Pay online at **IRS Direct Pay** (irs.gov/payments) — free, takes 5 minutes, no account needed.
- How much: easiest "safe harbor" rule — pay in at least **100% of what you owed in taxes last year** (110% if you earned over $150k), split into 4 equal chunks. First year freelancing with no prior-year baseline? Just pay 25-30% of each quarter's profit (from the savings account you've been building — see #2).
- Also check if your state has income tax and its own quarterly estimated payment system (most states with income tax do).

## 4. Track income & expenses without becoming a spreadsheet person
- Use **Wave (free)** or **QuickBooks Self-Employed (~$15-20/mo, worth it once you're busy)**. Connect it to your business bank account — it auto-pulls transactions.
- Once a month (put a recurring 20-minute calendar reminder), scroll through and categorize transactions. That's it. That's the bookkeeping.
- Snap a photo of every business receipt into the app the moment you get it (Wave and QBSE both have a phone app for this) — don't save paper receipts in a drawer, you won't do it later.

## 5. Know what counts as a deductible expense (design-specific)
Software subscriptions (Adobe, Figma, fonts), a portion of your home internet/phone if you work from home, a home office (if you have a dedicated space — use the simplified method: $5/sq ft up to 300 sq ft), computer/monitor/tablet purchases, courses or conferences, business insurance, portion of health insurance premiums if self-employed. Your bookkeeping app will have categories for all of these — just tag things correctly once a month.

## 6. Get an EIN and consider an LLC (optional, not urgent)
Not required to freelance, but:
- An **EIN** (free, 5 minutes on irs.gov) lets you use that instead of your SSN on client W-9 forms — safer.
- An **LLC** mainly protects your personal assets if you get sued; it doesn't change your taxes by default. Worth it once you have real income or liability exposure (e.g. contracts with bigger clients), not urgent in year one.

## 7. Year-end: let a professional file
Don't DIY the actual tax return your first year. Use a CPA or enrolled agent who handles freelancers/small business — expect $300-600. They'll take your Wave/QBSE report (income + categorized expenses) and file your Schedule C. This is the best money you'll spend: they'll catch deductions you don't know about and make sure you're not screwing up self-employment tax. After year one, you'll have a much better sense of what's involved, if you ever want to take more of it on yourself.

## 8. The whole system in one sentence
**Separate account → move 25-30% of every payment to a tax savings account → pay quarterly via IRS Direct Pay → auto-track transactions in Wave, categorize monthly → hire a CPA in the spring.**

Do those five things and you genuinely cannot get screwed — worst case, your CPA finds you saved a little too much or too little and adjusts next year's percentage.
