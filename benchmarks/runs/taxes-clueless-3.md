# Your freelance bookkeeping + tax system (US, first year)

You don't need to understand tax law. You need a system that makes the right numbers show up automatically, and four dates on a calendar. Here it is.

## Do this

**Step 1 — Open a second checking account, today, before any more client money arrives.**
Any free business or "just a second personal" checking account at your current bank works (Chase, Capital One, a local credit union — doesn't matter which). This is the single highest-leverage move: every dollar clients pay you lands here, and only here. Never mix it with your personal spending account.

**Step 2 — Every time you get paid, immediately move money out, same day:**
- **30% → a separate high-yield savings account**, labeled "taxes — do not touch." This covers federal income tax + self-employment tax (Social Security/Medicare — as a freelancer you pay both the employer and employee halves, ~15.3%, which is why this bites first-timers). 30% is a deliberately safe default for a first year with no other major income; if your total income this year is low (under ~$47k single), you could over-save slightly, but that's a good problem — refunds beat penalties.
- The remaining 70% is yours to spend/live on.
- Do this by standing rule, not memory: set a recurring calendar reminder "move 30% to tax savings" for the day after any invoice is typically paid, or better, ask your bank to auto-transfer 30% of every deposit over $50 into the savings account (most banks support a simple percentage or rule-based auto-transfer in their app).

**Step 3 — Track income and expenses with one free tool: a Google Sheet (or Wave, free software, if you want auto bank-sync).**
I'd default you to **Wave** (waveapps.com) — it's free, connects to your bank, and auto-categorizes. Set it up now:
1. Sign up with your own email.
2. Connect the business checking account from Step 1.
3. Let it pull transactions automatically. Every week (set a recurring 10-minute calendar block, e.g. Sunday 6pm), just glance through and confirm the categories it guessed — don't build anything from scratch.

If you'd rather not connect a bank to software, use a spreadsheet instead with two tabs, "Income" and "Expenses," columns: Date | Client/Vendor | Amount | Category | Notes. Either is fine — the point is *some* record exists, weekly, not perfect bookkeeping.

**Step 4 — Save every receipt for anything business-related, the moment you buy it.**
Software subscriptions (Figma, Adobe, fonts), a laptop or monitor, a portion of home internet, a co-working desk, business cards, a course on design — all deductible, meaning they reduce the income you're taxed on. Take a photo with your phone and email it to yourself with subject "biz expense," or drop it in a "Tax receipts 2026" folder in Google Drive/Photos the instant you buy something. Don't rely on finding it in your inbox in April — you won't.

**Step 5 — Pay estimated taxes four times a year (this is the part people get fined for skipping).**
The IRS wants you to pay as you go, not once in April, because you no longer have an employer withholding tax from a paycheck. Use **IRS Direct Pay** (irs.gov/payments/direct-pay) — free, no account needed, pay straight from your bank account. Dates for income earned in 2026:
- **Q1 (Jan–Mar earnings): due April 15, 2026**
- **Q2 (Apr–May earnings): due June 15, 2026**
- **Q3 (Jun–Aug earnings): due September 15, 2026**
- **Q4 (Sep–Dec earnings): due January 15, 2027**

How much to pay each quarter: log into your tracking tool, see total profit (income minus expenses) for that period, pay ~30% of that from your tax savings account. Put these four dates in your phone calendar right now, with a reminder one week before each — this is not optional admin, it's how you avoid a penalty.

**Step 6 — File your annual return by April 15, 2027 (for tax year 2026), using the numbers your tracker already has.**
Use **FreeTaxUSA.com** (federal filing free, state ~$15) or hand your Wave export / spreadsheet to a cheap local tax preparer ($150–400 for a simple freelance return) if you'd rather not touch a tax form at all — given you said you don't want to understand this, I'd default you to a local CPA/preparer for year one specifically, then switch to DIY software once you've seen a real return done correctly once. You'll file a Schedule C (business profit/loss) and Schedule SE (self-employment tax) alongside your normal Form 1040 — the software or preparer handles the actual forms; you just hand over the Wave export.

## Careful

- **Don't spend the 30% tax money, ever, for any reason.** It isn't yours — it's already the government's, you're just holding it. Spending it now means a shortfall (plus possible penalty) next April that can't be undone by working harder later.
- **Missing the quarterly payments (Step 5) triggers an underpayment penalty**, even if you pay everything correctly by April 15 the following year. It's a small percentage, not huge, but it's a completely avoidable cost — the calendar reminders exist for exactly this.
- **Mixing personal and business spending in one account (skipping Step 1) is the #1 first-year mistake.** It doesn't just look messy — it makes it genuinely hard to prove what's a legitimate business expense if the IRS ever asks, and costs you real deductions you'll simply forget about.
- **A contract without a late-payment/kill fee clause and getting paid before you finish are not tax issues, but they're the other way freelancers get screwed in year one** — worth a separate 10-minute fix (ask a client for 50% upfront) even though it's outside what you asked.

## Check it worked

Thirty days from now, open your tracking tool (Wave or the sheet) and confirm: every client payment that hit your business account this month has a matching row, and your tax savings account balance equals roughly 30% of total deposits. If either is missing or off, the system has a leak — usually a payment that skipped the second account, or a week you forgot to categorize. Fix the habit, not just the one entry.

## Decided for you

- **30% tax set-aside over a flat 25% or 20%** — because self-employment tax (15.3%) alone eats more than most first-timers expect, before federal or state income tax is even added; safer to over-save.
- **Wave over QuickBooks** — because it's free and does everything a solo freelancer with no employees needs; QuickBooks starts paying for itself only once you have payroll, inventory, or an accountant who specifically wants QuickBooks files.
- **IRS Direct Pay over mailing a check or using a third-party payment app** — because it's free (third-party processors charge ~2% "convenience fees"), immediate, and gives you a confirmation number as proof of payment.
- **A local paid preparer for year one over DIY software** — because a first Schedule C has judgment calls (home office %, equipment depreciation choices) that are cheap to get right once with a professional and easy to copy yourself in later years.
- **A second bank account over a "mental" separation of funds** — because a real account boundary is the only kind that survives a busy month; mental boundaries don't.

## Only you can decide

- **Which state you live in** — state income tax rules and rates vary a lot (some states have none). I assumed nothing about state tax here; when you set up FreeTaxUSA or talk to a preparer, they'll ask your state and handle it, but budget a bit extra beyond the 30% if your state has income tax (most do).
- **Whether you'll have a "day job" W-2 income too this year** — if part of this year you're also on payroll somewhere, that changes your safe-harbor math (how much you must pay in) and could lower how much you need to set aside. I assumed freelancing is your only income for these defaults; tell your preparer if it isn't.
- **Your total expected profit this year** — I can't size your actual tax bill without it. I assumed a "modest first-year freelancer" profile for the 30% default; if you expect to clear well over $100k profit, tell whoever files your return so they can check whether 30% is still enough (it usually still is, but worth a five-minute confirm).
