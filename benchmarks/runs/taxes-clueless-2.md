# Your freelance money system (set up once, 45 minutes)

You don't need to understand taxes. You need three habits and one spreadsheet. Here it is, fully built.

## Careful — read this part first

- **The IRS wants payments 4x a year, not once in April.** As a freelancer, nobody withholds tax from what you're paid, so you owe it yourself, quarterly. Miss this and you owe a penalty on top of the tax — not huge, but avoidable for zero cost. 2026 deadlines for 2025... wait, for income earned in 2025: Jan 15, 2026 is the last one for last year. For income you earn in 2026: Apr 15, Jun 15, Sep 15, 2026, and Jan 15, 2027.
- **Save 25–30% of every payment the day it lands.** Not at tax time — the day it lands. This is the single habit that prevents "I owe $6,000 and have $400" in April. Move it to a separate savings account immediately, treat it as already spent.
- **Mixing personal and business money in one bank account is the #1 way freelancers get screwed** — not by the IRS, but by themselves, six months from now, trying to remember if that $340 charge was a client lunch or your own dinner. Open a second checking account (free at any bank, takes 15 minutes) and run 100% of freelance income and expenses through it.
- **"I'll just remember it" is how deductions get lost.** Expenses you don't log within a week, you will not reconstruct in February. The system below fixes this with zero memory required.

## Do this

### 1. Open a dedicated business checking account (today)
Any free checking account works — doesn't need to be a "business account" legally for a sole proprietor (which is what you are by default, no paperwork needed). Use your own bank, 15 minutes online.
- All client payments go in here.
- All business expenses (software, a new monitor, client coffee, portion of internet/phone) come out of here.
- Never pay personal stuff from it.

### 2. Open a separate high-yield savings account labeled "TAXES — DO NOT TOUCH"
This is where the 25–30% goes. Setting it up:
- Any online bank (Marcus, Ally, Capital One 360 — doesn't matter which, they're all fine, pick whichever opens fastest).
- Set up **automatic transfer**: every time a client payment lands in the business checking account, move 28% of it to this savings account. Do it manually the same day if you can't automate it — but do it every single time, no exceptions, no "I'll top it up later."

**Why 28%:** for a typical first-year freelancer with no other major income, this covers roughly ~15% self-employment tax (Social Security + Medicare) plus ~10-15% federal income tax at modest income levels, leaving a little cushion. It's a deliberately safe overestimate — if you save too much, you get it back as a refund; if you save too little, you owe a penalty. Overestimating costs you nothing but a slightly fatter refund.

### 3. Track every dollar with one spreadsheet (I built the structure below — copy it)
Make a Google Sheet with two tabs:

**Tab 1: Income**
| Date | Client | Amount | Invoice # | Notes |

**Tab 2: Expenses**
| Date | What | Amount | Category | Business % | Notes |

Categories that matter for a designer, specifically:
- Software subscriptions (Adobe, Figma, Canva, fonts)
- Equipment (laptop, monitor, tablet, camera — over $2,500 gets depreciated, under that just expensed same year)
- Home office (see below — this one's a trap, handle carefully)
- Portion of phone/internet bill (business-use %, e.g. 30%)
- Professional development (courses, books)
- Marketing (website hosting, portfolio site, business cards)
- Contractor/subcontractor payments if you ever hire help
- Bank fees, accounting software (this one, basically)
- Travel to client meetings (mileage or actual cost)
- Health insurance premiums if you buy your own (this is a real, large deduction most freelancers miss)

**Log every expense within a week of paying for it.** Photograph the receipt with your phone and drop the image link or just a note in the sheet. You don't need perfect records, you need contemporaneous ones — "handled this week" beats "reconstructed in March" every time.

**Home office deduction — handle it, don't skip it, but don't overclaim it.** If you have a room or clearly defined area used *only* for work, you can deduct a percentage of rent/utilities equal to that area's share of your home's square footage. The simple version: IRS "simplified method" = $5 per square foot of office space, up to 300 sq ft ($1,500 max), no receipts needed. Use this one — it's the safe, simple default. Don't use the complicated actual-expense version unless an accountant sets it up for you.

### 4. File quarterly estimated taxes (Form 1040-ES)
Every quarter, look at your Income tab total for the quarter, subtract your Expenses tab total, and pay ~15.3% self-employment tax + your income tax bracket on the net. In practice for year one, easiest safe move:

- Use **IRS Direct Pay** (irs.gov/payments/direct-pay) — free, no account creation with third parties needed, pay straight from your bank account.
- If the math above feels like too much: pay 25-28% of that quarter's **net** income (income minus expenses) each quarter. This is a safe-default estimate, not exact, but "close and on time" beats "exact and late" — the penalty is based on underpayment, not on getting the number perfectly right.
- Also check your state — most states with income tax want quarterly estimated payments too. (Assumed: I don't know your state. See "Only you can decide" below.)

### 5. At year-end, use free/cheap software to actually file — don't wing the 1040
For a first year with income under roughly $60-80k, **FreeTaxUSA** (federal free, state ~$15) or **Cash App Taxes** (fully free) both correctly handle Schedule C (business income) and Schedule SE (self-employment tax) for a freelancer. TurboTax works too but charges more for self-employment. Feed it the totals from your two spreadsheet tabs — Income total, Expenses total by category.

## Check it worked

At the end of this month, look at your savings account labeled "TAXES." It should contain roughly 28% of everything you've been paid this month. If it's $0 and you've been paid, the automatic transfer isn't actually running — fix that today, not in December.

## Decided for you

- **28% savings rate over the "exact" calculation** — because the exact number depends on your total income, filing status, and state, none of which I have; 28% is a safe overestimate that avoids penalties and just means a bigger refund if wrong, rather than a scramble if under.
- **Simplified home-office method ($5/sq ft) over the actual-expense method** — the actual method needs precise utility/rent allocation and is a common audit-attention area; simplified is nearly as valuable and dramatically harder to get wrong.
- **A plain free checking + savings account over an actual "LLC" or business bank product** — you're a sole proprietor by default with no formation paperwork; forming an LLC doesn't reduce your taxes as a solo designer and adds cost and complexity you don't need in year one. (If you're worried about liability from client lawsuits, that's a real but separate question — see below.)
- **Google Sheets over paid bookkeeping software (QuickBooks etc.)** — at freelancer scale, a two-tab spreadsheet tracks everything an accountant needs; paid software earns its cost once you have multiple contractors or >$100k revenue, not before.
- **IRS Direct Pay over a third-party payment app** — it's free and goes straight to the IRS with no middleman fees.

## Only you can decide

- **Your state** — I assumed nothing about state taxes since I don't know where you live. Some states (TX, FL, WA, etc.) have no income tax and you can skip state quarterlies entirely; most others need a state equivalent of the 1040-ES. Tell me your state and I'll give exact quarterly steps.
- **Whether you want liability protection (an LLC)** — if you ever worry a client could sue you over a design mistake, an LLC ($50-500 depending on state, plus annual fees) separates your personal assets from business risk. I didn't set this up since it's a legal/cost tradeoff only you can weigh — assumed "not yet" for a first year.
- **Whether you'll clear roughly $40k+ this year** — if yes, get a real accountant for the year-end filing (not just software) since deduction strategy gets more valuable at that income; assumed you're starting smaller and software-filing is enough for now. Revisit this in Q4.
