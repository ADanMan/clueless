# Your freelance bookkeeping + tax system (US, year one)

You don't need to understand tax law. You need three habits and four dates. Here's the whole system.

## Do this

**Step 1 — Separate your money today (30 min, free, irreversible-ish so do it first)**
Open a free business checking account (Novo, Found, or Relay all work, no monthly fee, open online in ~15 min). Every client payment goes into this account. Every business expense (software, laptop, portion of internet, coworking) comes out of this account. Never mix personal spending through it. This single habit prevents 80% of freelancer tax pain.

**Step 2 — Set up the "tax bucket" (do this before you spend your first payment)**
Open a separate free high-yield savings account (same bank or Ally/Marcus). Every time a client payment lands, immediately move **30%** of it into this savings account. Don't touch it. That's your tax money — it is never yours to spend. This is the single most important habit in this whole system: freelancers who skip it are the ones who get a $6,000 tax bill they can't pay.

**Step 3 — Track income and expenses (pick one, don't overthink it)**
Use Bonsai, QuickBooks Self-Employed, or even a simple spreadsheet (template below) — log every payment in and every business expense out, with the date and what it was for. Spend 10 minutes a week, not a marathon in April.

Minimal spreadsheet columns: `Date | Client | Amount In | Category | Amount Out | Notes`

**Step 4 — Pay quarterly estimated taxes (this is the part freelancers don't know exists)**
As a freelancer, the IRS wants you to pay tax four times a year, not once. Miss this and you owe a penalty even if you pay in full by April. Use IRS Direct Pay (irs.gov/payments/direct-pay) — free, takes 5 minutes, no account needed.

2026 due dates for 2026 income:
- Jan 15, 2026 (for Q4 2025 income — if you earned anything in Q4 2025, this may already be due; if this is truly your first income, start with the next one)
- Apr 15, 2026
- Jun 15, 2026
- Sep 15, 2026
- Jan 15, 2027 (Q4 2026)

How much to pay each quarter: roughly **25–30% of what you were paid that quarter**. Since you're already moving 30% to savings as you go (Step 2), paying quarterly is just: log into IRS Direct Pay, send the amount sitting in that savings account for the quarter, done. Same for your state if your state has income tax (say which state and I'll give you the exact portal and rate).

**Step 5 — Keep every receipt for anything business-related**
Software subscriptions (Figma, Adobe, etc.), a portion of your laptop, a portion of internet/phone, client meeting meals (50% deductible), coworking/home office. Just forward receipts to a dedicated email folder or snap a photo into Bonsai/QuickBooks. You don't need to categorize perfectly — "close enough" now beats "perfect" never.

**Step 6 — File your annual return by April 15, 2027 (for 2026 income)**
Use FreeTaxUSA (federal free, state ~$15) or hand everything to a CPA who handles freelancers (~$300–600, often worth it for year one). You'll file a Schedule C (your business profit/loss) and Schedule SE (self-employment tax) alongside your regular 1040.

## Careful

- **The 30% tax bucket is not optional and not a "nice to have."** Self-employment tax (15.3%) plus income tax easily hits 25–30% of your net profit. If you spend that money, you cannot undo it — you'll owe it in April regardless, often with a penalty on top.
- **Missing quarterly payments has a real, compounding cost.** The IRS charges an underpayment penalty (currently roughly 8%/year, prorated) even if you pay everything by April 15. It's not catastrophic but it's pure waste.
- **Mixing personal and business money in one account is the #1 thing that makes an audit painful and a CPA expensive** — every bank statement becomes a forensic exercise. Keeping them separate from day one is free and irreversible in the good sense.
- **"1099 income" ≠ "money you keep."** If a client pays you $5,000, treat roughly $3,500 as actually yours and $1,500 as already the government's, sitting in your account. Reframing it this way now prevents the nasty surprise later.

## Check it worked

At the end of your first full quarter, open the tax savings account: the balance should be close to 30% of everything you were paid that quarter. If it's near zero, you've been spending your tax money — stop and fix Step 2 immediately, before you spend more.

## Decided for you

- **30% set-aside over the "actual" marginal rate** — because calculating your real rate requires knowing your total income for the year, which you don't know yet. 30% overshoots slightly for most first-year freelancers at modest income, which means a refund instead of a bill. Overshooting is the safe direction.
- **Novo/Found/Relay over a big bank's business account** — free, no minimum balance, opens in minutes online, built for freelancers. A traditional bank business account often has monthly fees and paperwork you don't need yet.
- **Spreadsheet or Bonsai over hiring a bookkeeper immediately** — at year one, income is usually simple enough that you can self-track; hire a bookkeeper once you're consistently billing $80k+/year or you're spending more than an hour a week on this.
- **Quarterly IRS Direct Pay over "just paying it all in April"** — paying all at once guarantees an underpayment penalty; quarterly avoids it entirely and costs you nothing extra in effort once the habit is set.
- **FreeTaxUSA/CPA over TurboTax** — TurboTax upcharges heavily for Schedule C (self-employment) filing; FreeTaxUSA does the same job for free federal / cheap state, and a freelancer-specific CPA is worth the money in year one specifically because they catch deductions you don't know exist.

## Only you can decide

- **Which state you're in** — state income tax rules and quarterly due dates vary (some states have no income tax at all: TX, FL, WA, NV, etc.). I assumed you have state tax; tell me your state and I'll give exact numbers.
- **Whether you formed (or want) an LLC or are just operating as yourself** — I assumed you're operating as a sole proprietor (the default, no paperwork needed, what almost all first-year freelancers are). If you've filed an LLC or S-corp election, the tax mechanics change and I'll adjust.
- **Your expected annual income** — I used 30% as a flat safe estimate. If you're on track to earn under ~$40k this year, your real rate is closer to 20–25% and you could set aside less; if you're unsure, 30% stays the safer default until you have a few months of data.
