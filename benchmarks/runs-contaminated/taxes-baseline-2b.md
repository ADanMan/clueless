# Do this

**1. Open a separate business checking account (this week).**
Any free business checking account works (Novo, Found, Bluevine, or just ask your current bank). Every payment you receive from clients goes into this account. Every business expense (software, laptop, portion of rent if you have a home office, etc.) comes out of it. Do not run personal spending through it. This single habit does 80% of the bookkeeping work for you automatically.

**2. Set aside 25–30% of every payment you receive, immediately.**
Open a separate savings account ("Taxes — do not touch") linked to the business checking account. The moment a client payment lands, move 25% of it (30% if you're in a higher-income state like CA or NY) into that savings account. This is not your money — treat it as already spent. This is the single thing that prevents "getting screwed": freelancers get burned not by tax complexity but by spending the tax money and not having it in April.

**3. Track income and expenses with one free tool: Wave (waveapps.com).**
Free, built for freelancers, connects to your bank account, auto-categorizes transactions. Sign up, connect your business checking account. Once a month (set a recurring 15-minute calendar reminder), log in and confirm the auto-categorized transactions look right. That's it — that's your bookkeeping.

**4. Pay quarterly estimated taxes.**
Because no employer is withholding tax for you, the IRS wants payments 4 times a year, not just once in April. Deadlines for 2026 tax year: **Jan 15, Apr 15, Jun 15, Sep 15** (and Jan 2027 for Q4). Use the IRS's **Direct Pay** tool (irs.gov/payments/direct-pay) — no account needed, pay straight from your bank account, free. Pay whatever has built up in your "Taxes — do not touch" account for that quarter. If you don't know the exact number, paying something close is far better than paying nothing — the penalty for underpaying a little is small; the penalty for paying nothing all year and owing a lump sum in April is much worse and can compound.

**5. Save every receipt digitally.**
Forward every business-related receipt (software subscriptions, a new monitor, Adobe/Figma, portion of internet bill, client lunches) to a dedicated email folder or just photograph it and dump it in a "2026 Tax Receipts" folder in Google Drive/Photos. You don't need to categorize them now — just don't lose them. These become deductions that lower what you owe.

**6. In January 2027, hire a tax preparer for your first return — don't DIY it.**
Your first year of self-employment income is exactly when a $150–400 CPA or enrolled agent (use a local one, or a service like Cash App Taxes for simple returns / a real CPA if income is over ~$40k) pays for itself many times over by catching deductions you don't know exist (home office, health insurance premiums, retirement contributions, mileage). Bring them the Wave export and your receipts folder. Do this every year going forward, not just year one.

# Careful

- **Spending the tax money is the #1 way freelancers get "screwed."** If you don't physically move 25–30% out of reach the day you get paid, you will spend it without realizing it, and April/quarterly deadlines become a crisis. Automate the transfer if your bank allows it.
- **Missing quarterly payments entirely** (not just paying late, but not knowing they exist) causes the biggest unpleasant surprises. Even an imperfect quarterly payment beats skipping it.
- **Mixing personal and business spending in one account** makes your books unreconstructable later and is the most common reason a CPA has to charge extra or a deduction gets missed. Keep them separate from day one — this is not optional.

# Check it worked

At the end of this month, log into Wave and confirm: (a) all your client payments show up as income, (b) your "Taxes" savings account balance equals roughly 25–30% of what you were paid this month. If both are true, the system is working.

# Decided for you

- Chose **Wave over QuickBooks/FreshBooks** because it's free and sufficient for a solo freelancer with simple income — no reason to pay monthly for software yet.
- Chose a **flat 25–30% set-aside rule over calculating your exact tax bracket** because you said you don't want to learn this — a slightly-over estimate is safer than a precise-but-fragile calculation you won't maintain.
- Chose **IRS Direct Pay over mailing checks or third-party apps** because it's free, direct, and has no signup friction.
- Chose **hiring a CPA for the actual tax return over DIY software (TurboTax Self-Employed etc.)** because first-year self-employment has enough nuance (self-employment tax, deductible expenses, possibly a home office deduction) that a professional's fee is usually smaller than the deductions they find.

# Only you can decide

- **What state you're in** — some states (CA, NY, etc.) also require state estimated tax payments on top of federal; I assumed you'd confirm this with your CPA. If you tell me your state, I can give you the exact additional step.
- **Whether you expect to earn over ~$47,000 net this year** — above that, self-employment tax planning gets more valuable to do mid-year rather than waiting until April; assumed this doesn't apply yet, but flag it if your income is trending high.
- **Whether you want a retirement account (e.g. a Solo 401k or SEP IRA)** — these reduce your taxable income and are worth opening before year-end, but I didn't set one up since it requires picking a brokerage; assumed "later" is fine for year one.
