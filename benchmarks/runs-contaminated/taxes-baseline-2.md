You asked for a simple bookkeeping/tax system — here it is, ready to use today.

## Do this

**1. Open a separate business checking account (this week).**
Any free business checking account works (Novo, Bluevine, or your existing bank's free small-business account). Every dollar a client pays you goes in here, and every business expense comes out of here. Nothing else touches this account. This one habit prevents 90% of tax headaches.

**2. Save for taxes automatically, every time you get paid.**
Freelancers don't have an employer withholding tax, so you have to do it yourself. The safe default: the moment a payment lands, move **30%** of it into a separate savings account labeled "TAXES — DO NOT TOUCH." If you're in a high-tax state (CA, NY, NJ) or expect to earn over ~$80k this year, use 35%. Yes, this will feel like too much. It isn't — refunds are for people who over-saved; a surprise bill in April is for people who didn't.

**3. Track income and expenses in one spreadsheet — set up now.**
You don't need software yet. Make a spreadsheet (Google Sheets is fine) with two tabs:
- *Income*: date, client, amount, date paid
- *Expenses*: date, vendor, amount, category (software, equipment, home office, travel, education, subscriptions), business purpose (one line)

Every time money moves in your business account, log it here within a week. Set a recurring 15-minute reminder every Sunday — "log this week's transactions." That's the entire bookkeeping system. When you're making over ~$3–5k/month, upgrade to Wave (free) or QuickBooks Self-Employed (~$20/mo), which can auto-import from your bank. Not needed on day one.

**4. Pay quarterly estimated taxes — mark these dates now.**
The IRS wants payment as you earn, not once a year. Miss this and you owe a penalty even if you pay in full by April. 2026 deadlines for 2026 income:
- Q1: **April 15, 2026**
- Q2: **June 15, 2026**
- Q3: **September 15, 2026**
- Q4: **January 15, 2027**

How much: roughly the 30% you've been setting aside for that quarter's income, paid via IRS Direct Pay (irs.gov/payments, no account needed, free) plus your state's equivalent if your state has income tax. First quarter, estimate low-ish is fine — you true it up on your tax return.

**5. Keep every receipt for anything you buy for work.**
Laptop, software subscriptions (Figma, Adobe), a portion of your phone bill, a home-office percentage of rent/utilities if you have a dedicated workspace, courses, a portion of health insurance premiums. Snap a photo and drop it in a "Receipts 2026" folder in Google Drive, named `YYYY-MM-DD-vendor-amount`. These reduce what you owe — every unlogged receipt is money you're donating to the IRS for no reason.

**6. File a Schedule C and Schedule SE next April, with a real preparer the first year.**
As a sole proprietor (the default — you haven't formed an LLC, so this is automatically your status), your freelance income goes on Schedule C, and you pay self-employment tax (Social Security + Medicare, ~15.3%) on top of regular income tax via Schedule SE. For year one, pay a CPA or use a service like TurboTax Self-Employed / a local accountant (~$300–600) rather than DIYing it — they'll catch deductions you don't know exist and set you up correctly for future years.

## Careful

- **Don't spend the tax savings account.** It will sit there looking like free money. It isn't yours — treat it as already spent.
- **Don't mix personal and business spending in one account.** If you buy a coffee with the business card "just this once," it turns into every time, and by tax time you can't tell what's deductible. Keep the wall solid from day one.
- **Missing a quarterly payment isn't the end of the world**, but ignoring it for multiple quarters compounds penalties. If you miss one, pay it as soon as you remember rather than waiting for the next deadline.

## Check it worked

At the end of this month, your spreadsheet's Income tab total plus your bank statement's deposits should match, and your tax savings account balance should equal roughly 30% of what you've been paid. If either doesn't match, something didn't get logged — go find it before it becomes a habit.

## Decided for you

- Chose a spreadsheet over paid software for now, because at low volume it's free and forces you to actually look at every transaction once — you'll know when you've outgrown it.
- Chose 30% tax set-aside over a lower number, because underpaying triggers penalties and a scary April bill; overpaying just means a refund.
- Chose "pay a CPA for year one" over full DIY, because the cost (~$300–600) is usually smaller than the deductions a professional finds that you'd miss, and you explicitly don't want to become an expert in this.
- Chose Schedule C / sole proprietor over forming an LLC or S-corp, because that's your default status with zero paperwork, and entity changes only pay off once you're consistently earning well above typical first-year freelance income.

## Only you can decide

- **Your state** — I assumed you may owe state income tax on top of federal; if you're in a no-income-tax state (TX, FL, WA, etc.), skip the state estimated payments. Confirm your state.
- **Home office setup** — I assumed you have a dedicated space you could deduct a portion of rent/utilities for; if you work from a shared/non-dedicated space, skip that deduction (it has stricter rules and isn't worth the audit risk if it doesn't clearly qualify).
- **Expected total 2026 income** — I assumed mid-range freelance income (under ~$80k) for the 30% estimate; if you expect a notably higher or lower income, the percentage should be adjusted — a CPA can nail this down in one conversation.
